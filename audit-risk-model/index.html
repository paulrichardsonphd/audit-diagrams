How much evidence does the audit need? The audit risk model answers that in planning. Pick the risks, see what happens to planned detection risk, and learn what moves each one.

Solve for the one the auditor controls: PDR = AAR ÷ (IR × CR)

IR × CR = the risk of material misstatement (RoMM). It exists before the audit starts; the auditor can only assess it. PDR is solved from the other three: lower PDR → more evidence.

Key terms
Acceptable audit risk
Acceptable audit risk is a measure of how willing the auditor is to accept that the financial statements may be materially misstated after the audit is completed and an unmodified opinion has been issued.In plain terms: how much risk the firm will accept of signing off on F/S that are still materially wrong. The firm sets it; the whole model works back from it.Source: Arens et al., Ch. 9 (exact wording)
Inherent risk
Inherent risk measures the auditor’s assessment of the susceptibility of an assertion about a class of transactions, account balance, or disclosure to material misstatement, before considering the effectiveness of related internal controls.In plain terms: how likely something is to be wrong in the first place, before any controls.Source: Arens et al., Ch. 9 (exact wording)
Control risk
Control risk measures the auditor’s assessment of the risk that a material misstatement could occur in an assertion and not be prevented or detected and corrected on a timely basis by the client’s internal controls.In plain terms: the chance the client’s controls miss a material misstatement.Source: Arens et al., Ch. 9 (exact wording)
Planned detection risk
Planned detection risk is the risk that audit evidence for an audit objective will fail to detect misstatements exceeding performance materiality.In plain terms: the chance our own procedures miss it. Lower PDR means more evidence.Source: Arens et al., Ch. 9 (exact wording)
Risk of material misstatement
The risk of material misstatement is the risk that the financial statements contain a material misstatement due to fraud or error prior to the audit.In plain terms: the odds the F/S management hands the auditor already contain a material misstatement. In the model, RoMM = IR × CR.Source: Arens et al., Ch. 8 (exact wording)
Audit risk
The risk that the auditor will conclude after conducting an adequate audit that the financial statements are fairly stated and an unmodified opinion can therefore be issued when, in fact, they are materially misstated.In plain terms: the chance the auditor signs off and a material misstatement is still in there. Acceptable audit risk is how much of this the firm is willing to accept.Source: Arens et al., glossary (exact wording)

What moves each risk

Acceptable audit risk · the firm decides

The more is riding on the audit, the lower the firm sets AAR

  • External users rely heavily on the F/S → AAR ↓Signs: a large company, widely held (public), large amounts of debt.
  • The client may have financial trouble after the report is issued → AAR ↓Signs: weak liquidity, recent losses, growth financed with debt, a risky industry, weak management.
  • Management’s integrity is questionable → AAR ↓If the firm takes the client at all, it accepts less risk and gathers more evidence.
  • Few outside users, healthy finances, honest management → AAR ↑The firm can accept more risk, so the audit needs less evidence.
Inherent risk · the auditor assesses

The more room for error before any controls, the higher IR

Such assessments are typically based on discussions with management, knowledge of the company, and results in audits of previous years. (Arens et al., Ch. 9)

  • Nature of the client’s business → IR ↑Fast-changing products, inventory that goes obsolete, a volatile industry.
  • Misstatements in prior audits → IR ↑Errors found last year tend to show up again.
  • First-year (initial) engagement → IR ↑No prior-year audit experience with this client.
  • Related parties and nonroutine transactions → IR ↑Unusual, one-off, or non-arm’s-length deals are easy to get wrong.
  • Judgment needed to record the amount → IR ↑Estimates such as the allowance for doubtful accounts, warranties, goodwill.
  • Makeup of the population → IR ↑A few very large or unusual items, or amounts due from related parties.
  • Fraud risk factors → IR ↑Incentives to misstate the F/S, or assets that are easy to steal, like cash.
  • Routine, simple, repeat client with no past problems → IR ↓Recurring transactions recorded the same way every year.
Control risk · the auditor assesses

CR depends on how well the client’s controls work

  • Effective controls, and the auditor tested them → CR ↓Tests of controls are required to assess CR below the maximum.
  • Controls look reasonable, limited testing → CR moderateSome reliance on controls, supported by some tests of controls.
  • Weak controls, no controls, or the auditor chooses not to test them → CR at maximumCommon at small private clients. No reliance on controls, so substantive tests carry the load.

Assessing control risk is the Ch 11–12 work: understand the controls, decide whether to rely on them, then test them.

Planned detection risk · the auditor solves for

PDR isn’t assessed. It falls out of the other three

Lower PDR means the audit has to catch more, so the auditor plans more evidence. That changes:

  • Nature: more reliable evidenceFor example, confirm with an outside party instead of relying on inquiry.
  • Timing: closer to year endSubstantive tests at the balance sheet date instead of at interim.
  • Extent: more of itLarger samples.
  • Staffing: more experienced peopleHigher-risk areas go to more experienced staff.

AAR and PDR move opposite to evidence. IR and CR move with it.

Keep straight

RoMM is not audit riskRoMM (IR × CR) is the risk before the audit. Audit risk is the risk that remains after it.
The firm sets AARNot the client. It’s a judgment about how much is riding on the audit.
No testing, no relianceCR can be assessed below the maximum only if the auditor tests the controls.
PDR follows from the other threeIt changes only when AAR, IR, or CR changes. The auditor then meets it through the evidence gathered: lower PDR, more evidence.
It’s a planning toolThe levels are judgments, usually Low, Moderate, or High. If evidence shows a risk is higher than planned, the auditor revises the model and gathers more evidence.
Inverse vs. directAAR and PDR are inversely related to evidence. IR and CR are directly related to it.
