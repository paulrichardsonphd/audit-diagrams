Ridgeline Motors' year ends 12/31, the audit report is dated Feb 5, and the 10-K is filed Feb 6. When something happens, ask: does it relate to the 12/31 F/S, and did we learn about it before or after the report? Pick a scenario.

Definition

Subsequent events

“However, events or transactions sometimes occur subsequent to the balance-sheet date, but prior to the issuance of the financial statements, that have a material effect on the financial statements and therefore require adjustment or disclosure in the statements. These occurrences hereinafter are referred to as ‘subsequent events.’”Source: PCAOB AS 2801.01 (exact wording)

In plain terms: two types. If the condition already existed at 12/31 (A, C), adjust the F/S (AS 2801.03). If the condition arose after 12/31, don’t adjust, but disclose it if leaving it out would mislead (AS 2801.05). The auditor’s subsequent events work runs to the report date (AS 2801.10).

Definition

Subsequent discovery of facts

“The procedures described in this section should be followed by the auditor who, subsequent to the date of the report upon audited financial statements, becomes aware that facts may have existed at that date which might have affected the report had he or she then been aware of such facts.”Source: PCAOB AS 2905.01 (exact wording)

In plain terms: the fact had to exist at the report date; the auditor just didn’t know. Something brand new after the report date doesn’t count. If it would have changed the report and people are relying on the F/S, the client revises the F/S and the auditor reissues the report (AS 2905.05–.06).

What to watch for: bankruptcies, major catastrophes (hurricane, tornado, war), lawsuits
How auditors find out: read the news, Google alerts for the client & its customers, social media
The audit covers the F/S & notes (Item 8), but auditors must read the entire 10-K for consistency
